## RED RIDING HOOD (Czerwony Kapturek)

**Role**: Protagonist.

**Source facts**
- A little girl living at the edge of the forest.
- Always wears a red cap; everyone calls her by it.
- Sent by her mother with a basket of medicine and treats to her sick grandmother in the middle of the forest.
- Waves goodbye to her mother and sets off.
- Walks fast along the forest path.
- Remembers grandmother loves flowers; reasons that a brief stop will do no harm.
- Leaves the path, picks flowers, drifts far from road and meadow without noticing.
- Meets the wolf; trusts him immediately because he is polite; tells him she is carrying medicine for her sick grandmother and explains exactly where grandmother lives.
- Arrives at the cottage, knocks, enters, sits down beside the bed.
- Notices grandmother looks somehow different from usual.
- Asks about the eyes, the ears ("worried"), the teeth ("surprised").
- Is swallowed.
- Comes out of the belly whole and healthy; embraces and thanks the forester; is escorted home.
- From then on never strays from the path when visiting grandmother.

**Proposed interpretation**
- Age: 8–9. Old enough to be trusted with the errand, young enough to be enchanted.
- Personality: warm, quick, talkative, practical, a self-narrator who thinks out loud — which is exactly why she talks herself off the path and tells the wolf too much.
- Objective: deliver the medicine and be the kind of person who can be trusted with it.
- Deeper need: to be treated as capable.
- Greatest fear: disappointing her mother. (Note: this is what makes the broken promise hurt, and what she must confess at the end.)
- Internal contradiction: she wants to prove she can follow the rule, and breaks it *in order to do the errand better* — the bouquet is an act of love.
- Strength: she notices. Weakness: she explains herself to anyone who asks nicely.
- Speech: full sentences, running commentary, "well, but —" constructions.
- Mannerism: touches the knot of her cap under her chin when uncertain.

**Arc**
Trusted → tempted → self-justifying → lost → disastrously candid → uneasy →
frightened → brave enough to ask → rescued → honest → choosing the path freely.
She ends where she began, but the rule is now hers rather than her mother's.

**Visual identifiers**: Red woollen cap, tied under the chin — the only strongly
saturated red in the film. Pale linen dress, dark pinafore, scuffed boots.
Wicker basket with a folded cloth.

**Continuity**
- The cap is never removed on screen until the cottage; it is knocked askew by the leap (3.2.2.2.1) and retied in 4.1.2.1.3.
- The basket: full in Part I, carried in the crook of the left arm; after the meadow it also holds the bouquet, which crowds the cloth aside.
- Pinafore acquires grass stains and a burr at the meadow (2.2.1.2.1) that remain for the rest of the film.
- Hem dampens crossing the stream landmark (2.1.1.1.2).

## THE WOLF (Wilk)

**Role**: Antagonist.

**Source facts**
- Appears suddenly in front of the girl when she turns around.
- Greets her politely — "Good day, little girl!" — and by politeness immediately wins her trust.
- Asks where she is going.
- Expresses sympathy about grandmother's illness, smiles slyly, says goodbye, and disappears as quickly as he appeared.
- Finds the cottage without difficulty.
- Knocks and enters before hearing an answer.
- Must act fast because the girl is already approaching.
- Swallows the grandmother whole, without long deliberation.
- Puts on her cap and glasses; jumps under the quilt at the last moment.
- Lies with only his head showing.
- Is briefly surprised by the first question, then answers all three.
- "To eat you!" — leaps from the bed and swallows the girl.
- Returns under the quilt and falls asleep.
- Snores loudly.
- Is dealt with by the forester "in short order".

**Proposed interpretation**
- Never comic, never cackling. His register is a well-mannered adult's — warm, unhurried, slightly too interested.
- Objective: eat. Method: ask.
- The sly smile is the only crack in the performance, and the girl does not see it; the audience does.
- He is physically enormous in close quarters and almost invisible in the open wood.
- Speech: courteous, formal, uses endearments ("kochanie", "moja droga wnusiu") that curdle on repetition.
- The one moment of genuine improvisation is the first question — he is caught out, pauses, then recovers. This is his only vulnerability and it should be legible.

**Arc**: Opportunist → strategist → impersonator → predator → complacent sleeper
→ undone by his own satisfied snoring. The story's logic: the appetite that makes
him dangerous is what puts him to sleep.

**Visual identifiers**: Grey-brown, bulky, deliberate. In disguise: grandmother's
white ruffled nightcap and small round spectacles over a muzzle, under a heavy
quilt — absurd and horrifying at once. Keep the full body hidden until the leap.

**Continuity**
- Nightcap sits wrong from the moment he puts it on; one ear not tucked in.
- The spectacles catch the window light so the eyes are not readable until 3.2.2.1.1.
- Grandmother's glasses are later found intact (4.1.2.1.2) — they must survive.
- Never shown full-figure between 3.1.1.1.3 and 3.2.2.2.1.

## GRANDMOTHER (Babcia)

**Role**: The errand's object; first victim.

**Source facts**
- Lives in a cottage in the middle of the forest.
- Is ill and in bed.
- Loves flowers (per the girl's recollection).
- Owns a nightcap and glasses.
- Is swallowed whole without a word of dialogue in the text.
- Emerges from the belly whole and healthy; embraces and thanks the forester.

**Proposed interpretation**
- Age: 70s. Lives alone, by choice, deep in the wood; competent, dry-humoured, unsentimental — which makes her helplessness in the bed land harder.
- Objective in her one conscious scene: to deal with whoever is at the door while barely able to sit up.
- The film gives her a brief pre-swallowing beat (3.1.2.2.1): she hears the knock, says "it's open", expects her granddaughter, and realises a half-second too late. **Proposed change — requires approval** (the source gives her no reaction).
- After the rescue she is notably more concerned about the girl than herself.

**Visual identifiers**: White ruffled nightcap, small round spectacles, heavy
patterned quilt, grey plait. A room full of dried flowers in jars — the planted
justification for the bouquet.

**Continuity**: Spectacles and nightcap transfer to the wolf and back. The quilt's
pattern must be identical in 3.1.2.2.1, 3.2.1.1.2 and 4.2.1.1.1.

## MOTHER (Mama)

**Role**: Sender; source of the rule.

**Source facts**
- Sends Red Riding Hood to the sick grandmother.
- Warned her to go straight along the road to grandmother and not turn off it.
- Is waved goodbye.

**Proposed interpretation**
- Age: early 30s. Capable, tired, running a smallholding alone. Her warning is not nagging; it is the one thing she can send with her daughter that isn't in the basket.
- Objective: get the medicine to her mother without putting her daughter at risk — and she cannot do both.
- Greatest fear: the forest.
- Internal contradiction: she gives the rule because she is afraid, and she lets her daughter go because she is more afraid of grandmother dying.
- She does not appear again until the gate at the end. **Proposed**: a wordless reunion rather than a scolding.

**Visual identifiers**: Grey-blue apron, hair bound in a kerchief, flour or herb
stains. The red thread she uses to repair the cap in 1.1.1.1.2 is the film's
colour motif in miniature.

**Continuity**: Appears in 1.1.1.1.2 – 1.1.1.2.3 and 4.2.2.1.1. Same apron, now
clean, at the gate.

## THE FORESTER (Gajowy)

**Role**: Rescuer. Often translated "woodcutter"; the source word is *gajowy* —
a forester or game-keeper, a man whose job is to watch the wood.

**Source facts**
- Happened to be passing near grandmother's cottage.
- From a distance saw the door was not properly closed and heard loud snoring.
- Wondered: "Is that grandmother snoring like that?"
- Looked in through the window out of curiosity; saw the wolf sleeping soundly in the bed.
- Guessed what had happened and dealt with the wolf in short order.
- Was embraced and thanked.
- Escorted the girl home.

**Proposed interpretation**
- Age: 40s–50s. Unhurried, observant, undramatic. His heroism is entirely composed of *paying attention to a door*.
- Objective: find out what is wrong at a house he passes every week.
- He is not a warrior; the dispatch is practical, brief and off-screen.
- Speech: few words, mostly questions. He asks the girl nothing accusatory on the walk home.
- **Proposed**: he is seen once early, in the background of 2.1.1.1.2, so his arrival is not a deus ex machina. Requires approval — the source introduces him only at the end.

**Visual identifiers**: Green-brown coat, broad hat, axe carried over the
shoulder, dog whistle on a cord (dog never seen — optional).

**Continuity**: Hat on throughout except in the cottage. The axe is never shown
striking.
