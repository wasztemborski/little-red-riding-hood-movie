# Document A — Adaptation Overview

**Last updated**: 2026-10-28

---

## Working title

**THE RED CAP** (*Czerwony Kapturek*)

## Source book details

- Title: *Czerwony Kapturek* ("Little Red Riding Hood")
- Attribution in source: "według Charlesa Perraulta" (after Charles Perrault)
- Form: short illustrated-book prose retelling, ~700 words, 8 paragraphs
- File: `00-inbox/story.md`
- Public domain folk material; this specific Polish retelling is paraphrased, not reproduced
- Variant note: ending follows the Grimm rescue, not Perrault's unrescued ending

## Logline

Sent alone through the deep forest to bring medicine to her sick grandmother, a
small girl in a red cap steps off the path for a handful of flowers — and the
wolf who meets her there reaches the cottage first.

## Short synopsis

A girl known by her red cap is sent by her mother to carry medicine and food to
her ill grandmother, who lives deep in the forest. Warned to keep to the path,
she is tempted by a meadow of flowers and strays. A courteous wolf finds her
there and, by simply asking kindly, learns exactly where the grandmother lives.
He arrives first, swallows the old woman, dresses in her cap and glasses and
waits in her bed. The girl arrives, senses that something is wrong, and asks
three questions about his eyes, his ears, his teeth. He swallows her too, and
falls asleep. A forester passing the cottage notices the half-open door and the
thunderous snoring, looks through the window, and understands. He deals with the
wolf; grandmother and girl climb out alive. He walks the girl home. She never
leaves the path again.

## Full narrative synopsis

**Part I.** Dawn at a cottage on the forest's edge. The girl's world is small,
bright and bounded: the yard, the gate, the treeline beyond. Her mother packs a
basket — a stoppered bottle of medicine, bread, a honeycomb, a cloth — and ties
the red cap under her chin. Grandmother is ill; the cottage is an hour deep in
the woods. The instruction is plain and repeated: *the path, straight there, no
turning off*. The girl promises. She goes through the gate and the trees close
over her.

**Part II.** The forest is beautiful before it is frightening. The girl walks
well and fast, narrating the errand to herself. Then light opens to her left:
a meadow full of flowers. She reasons herself out of the promise — grandmother
loves flowers; a moment costs nothing. She steps off. The picking draws her
further, flower by better flower, until the meadow itself is behind her and the
path is nowhere. When the bouquet is finished and she turns on her heel, the
wolf is standing there.

**Part III.** He is polite. That is his whole method. *Good day, little girl.
Where are you going?* She tells him everything, including the way. He is sorry
to hear grandmother is ill, smiles, and is gone as fast as he came. He finds the
cottage without difficulty, knocks, enters before an answer, and swallows the
grandmother whole — he must be quick, the girl is already on the path. He puts on
the cap and the glasses and gets under the quilt at the last second. The girl
knocks, comes in, and sits down beside a grandmother with only her head showing,
who looks somehow *wrong*. Big eyes — to see you better. Big ears — to hear you
better. Sharp teeth — **to eat you**. He leaps. Afterwards he crawls back under
the quilt and sleeps.

**Part IV.** A forester is passing. From a distance he sees the door standing
ajar and hears snoring loud enough to carry. *Does grandmother snore like that?*
He looks through the window: a wolf asleep in the bed. He guesses what happened,
goes in, and makes short work of him. Out of the wolf's belly climb grandmother
and the girl, whole and unharmed. They embrace the forester and thank him for
their lives. He walks the girl home through the evening forest, past the meadow,
on the path. From then on, whenever she visits her grandmother, she does not
leave the path — she has learned that keeping to the given road spares a great
deal of trouble.

## Genre and tone

**Genre**: Family fairy-tale adventure with a controlled dark fantasy register.
**Tone**: Bright, warm and tactile in Part I; wondrous then disorienting in
Part II; genuinely suspenseful and uncanny in Part III; cathartic and gentle in
Part IV. The model is a folk tale told well to a child who is old enough to be
frightened and young enough to be comforted. Menace is built through sound,
silence, scale and framing — never through gore.

## Central themes

1. **The path and the promise.** Rules given by love versus the honest pull of beauty.
2. **Politeness as camouflage.** The wolf's weapon is courtesy; danger is well-mannered.
3. **Telling too much.** The disaster is caused by information freely given, not by force.
4. **Noticing.** The girl's three questions are the story's act of courage: she registers that something is wrong and says so out loud.
5. **Rescue and second chances.** The forest is not forgiving by itself; community is.
6. **Smallness.** A child, a basket, and a very large wood.

## Subthemes and motifs

- Red against green and grey — the cap as the only saturated colour in the forest
- The basket's contents as a countdown of the errand's integrity
- Doors and thresholds: the gate, the cottage door left ajar, the quilt's edge
- Flowers: gift, temptation, and finally a wilted bouquet on a bedside table
- Sound as truth: birdsong, then silence, then snoring

## Target audience

Primary: families with children aged 7–12. Secondary: adult audiences for
classical fairy-tale cinema. Target certificate: PG / 7+. The swallowing and the
rescue are staged with shadow, sound and off-screen cutaway, with no blood.

## Recommended film format and runtime

**Recommended: animated or live-action-hybrid feature, 88–95 minutes.**
Planning target: **92 minutes**.

The source is ~700 words, which would support a 10-minute short. A feature is
only honest if the expansion material is *dramatisation of what the text
implies* — the journey, the temptation, the wolf's race ahead, the girl's
growing unease — rather than invented subplots. A miniseries is **not**
recommended: there is not enough story, and padding would dilute the tale's
single clean moral spine. If a shorter form is preferred, a **40-minute special**
is a strong alternative; the structure below collapses cleanly to it by cutting
Sections 2.2 and 4.2.

## Core adaptation principles

1. **Nothing in the source is cut.** Every one of the eight paragraphs is on screen, in order.
2. **Expansion only by dramatisation.** New screen time comes from staging moments the text states in a single clause (the walk, the picking, the wolf's run, the unease) — not from new plot.
3. **No new major characters.** Five characters, as in the source.
4. **The ending is untouched.** Rescue, thanks, escort home, the lesson.
5. **The three questions are sacred.** Eyes, ears, teeth — in that order, with the source's answers.
6. **The wolf is never comic.** He is courteous and then he is not.
7. **Chronology is linear.** No flashbacks, no voice-over narration, no framing device.
8. **Violence is implied.** The forester's dispatch of the wolf is a cutaway; the rescue is bloodless, as the source states ("całe i zdrowe" — whole and healthy).
9. **The moral is spoken by action, not a lecture.** The final image carries it.
10. **Interpretation is labelled.** Anything not in the text is marked in Document F.
